Here is the formal technical specification document for your custom automated truss cell build, optimized for rigidity and precision based on our design iterations.

## Structural Chassis & Frame

* **Base Frame:** A 1000 mm x 1000 mm square perimeter constructed from laser-cut steel plates (5 inches wide by 2 inches thick profile). A central steel cross-member spans the middle to support the primary drive mechanism.
* **Y-Axis Bridge (Plate B):** A 1000 mm long by 4 inch wide aluminum span. To eliminate vertical deflection and vibration, this plate must be reinforced by upgrading to 8 mm thickness, folding the edges into a C-channel, or bolting a 20x40 mm aluminum extrusion spine to the rear.
* **Z-Axis Interface:** A rigid, heavy-duty 90-degree cast aluminum corner gusset or thick steel L-bracket rigidly mates the moving Y-axis effector plate to the vertical Z-axis base plate.

## Linear Motion Kinematics

* **X-Axis (Gantry Base):** Dual 1000 mm MGN12H linear guide rails mounted on the outer steel perimeter plates to resist torsional twisting, driven centrally by a single 1000 mm SFU1204 ballscrew.
* **Y-Axis (Bridge Traverse):** A single 1000 mm MGN12H rail and 1000 mm SFU1204 ballscrew mounted parallel (side-by-side) on the reinforced Plate B.
* **Z-Axis (Vertical Drop):** A single 300 mm to 500 mm MGN12H rail and matched SFU1204 ballscrew to control the winding head's vertical stroke.

## Actuation & Drive Systems

* **Kinematic Drives (X, Y, Z):** NEMA 17 stepper motors (4.8 kg-cm holding torque, D-Type shaft, 200 steps/rev) directly coupled to the ballscrews without microstepping.
* **Winding Head:** NEMA 8 stepper motor (0.04 N·m) operating through a 4:1 mechanical reduction, utilizing a 4000 CPR quadrature encoder for precise closed-loop tracking.
* **Tooling Auxiliary:** TowerPro MG996R digital servo for 190° total gripper yaw, and a 12V 10 RPM self-locking worm gear reduction motor for the rotating cage indexer.

## Assembly & Alignment Protocol

* **Fastening Strategy:** All linear rails, ballscrew end mounts (BK10/BF10), and ball nut housings (DSG12H) must be bolted using standard M3 and M4 machine screws into laser-cut pilot holes tapped directly into the steel.
* **Chemical Locking:** Apply removable blue threadlocker (e.g., Loctite 242) to all fasteners; using epoxy adhesive directly on precision motion hardware is strictly prohibited to allow for future maintenance.
* **Rail Calibration:** Utilize the "master and slave" alignment method, securing one rail completely, then manually gliding the gantry carriage back and forth to dynamically align the loose secondary rail before final tightening.

## PART A — WHAT IS WRONG OR THIN IN THE DOCUMENT ABOVE

Ordered by how much it costs to find out late.

### A1. The base frame as written is 253 kg and cannot be laser-cut

"laser-cut steel plates (5 inches wide by 2 inches thick)" is 127 × 50.8 mm
in solid mild steel. One 1 m length of that is

    1000 × 127 × 50.8 mm³ × 7.85 g/cm³ = 50.6 kg

and the frame calls for five of them (four sides plus the cross-member):
**253 kg of steel** for a machine whose moving mass is 3.7 kg **(measured:
the mass of everything riding the carriage in the MuJoCo cell)**. Fibre
lasers in most job shops stop at 20–25 mm in mild steel **(estimated)**, so
50.8 mm would have to be plasma, waterjet or oxy-fuel — none of which hold
the edge tolerance the "tapped pilot holes" strategy below assumes.

This reads like a units slip: 5 inch × 2 inch is a **section**, not a plate
thickness. What the machine actually needs is a closed section in bending,
and two options are equivalent:

| option | I about the bending axis | mass, 1 m |
|---|---|---|
| 50 × 50 × 3 mm mild steel box | ≈ 208 000 mm⁴ | 4.4 kg |
| 40 × 80 aluminium T-slot extrusion | ≈ 600 000 mm⁴ (check the profile) | 2.6 kg |

Either gives the same midspan stiffness as the other (steel's E is 2.9× that
of aluminium, the extrusion's I is 2.9× the box's), at **one twentieth the
mass** and a fraction of the cost. A solid plate is the wrong shape for
bending stiffness at any thickness: all the material is near the neutral
axis.

### A2. The Y bridge sags 3 mm and rings at 11 Hz

This is the one that would have wasted a build. Take the specified 1000 × 100
× 8 mm aluminium plate, simply supported, carrying the Z assembly at midspan.
The Z assembly is a NEMA 17, a 500 mm screw, a 500 mm rail, the gusset and
the head — **≈ 3 kg (estimated)**, since the repo models only the tool masses
(ring 18 g, gripper 86 g, dispenser 65 g).

    I = 100 × 8³ / 12                     = 4 267 mm⁴
    δ_load = P L³ / 48 E I = 30 × 10⁹ / (48 × 69 000 × 4 267)  = 2.1 mm
    δ_self = 5 w L³ / 384 E I                                   = 0.9 mm
                                                          total ≈ 3 mm

Three millimetres of sag on a machine whose ballscrews repeat to 0.05 mm
**(measured: `Gantry.REPEAT`)**. Worse is the dynamics:

    k = 48 E I / L³ = 14.1 N/mm  →  f₁ = (1/2π)√(k/m) ≈ 11 Hz

The plan assumes every move settles in **150 ms** **(measured:
`Gantry.SETTLE_S`)**. An 11 Hz structure with light damping rings for of
order a second. Every one of the 799 ops in the build would wait on it, and
the two "axes settle" timeouts already visible in simulation are the ideal-
axis version of the same complaint.

To hold the sag under one REPEAT (0.05 mm) from the load alone:

    I ≥ P L³ / (48 E δ) = 30 × 10⁹ / (48 × 69 000 × 0.05) = 181 000 mm⁴

A 20 × 40 T-slot is of order 8–10 × 10⁴ mm⁴ about its strong axis
**(estimated — check the profile's datasheet)**, i.e. **about half of what is
needed**. So of the three options the document offers — 8 mm plate, folded
C-channel, or a 20 × 40 spine — **none of them is sufficient and they should
not be presented as alternatives**. Specify one part that meets the number:
a 40 × 80 extrusion, or a 40 × 40 pair, with the rail and screw on machined
pads. Then f₁ lands near 130 Hz **(estimated)** and the 150 ms settle is
real.

### A3. Two actuators cannot hold the tolerances the design is built on

These are not "upgrade later" items. Two of the design's headline accuracy
results rest on them.

**The cage indexer.** `JGY370-12V-10RPM` is a brushed DC worm motor with **no
feedback of any kind**. The repository asserts the cage indexes to
**`Cage.THETA_TOL = 0.05°` (measured)**, and the whole architecture rests on
the sentence "GEOMETRY IS HELD BY THE FIXTURE, NOT THE ROBOT" — which is only
true if the fixture returns to the same angle every time. An open-loop DC
motor with a worm will overshoot by degrees. Either
  * a stepper through the same worm (a NEMA 17 at 200 steps/rev through a
    30:1 worm is 0.06°/step — still not 0.05° repeatability, but bounded), or
  * **a hard detent per station**: the cage has only a handful of indexed
    angles per truss, and a spring-loaded plunger into a reamed hole is
    repeatable to a hundredth of a degree for pennies. This is the cheaper
    and better answer, and it matches the design's own philosophy — the V-pins
    and the over-centre knees are all kinematic locators rather than servos.

**The gripper yaw.** `MG996R` is a hobby servo: **±1° or worse, with gear
backlash of the same order (estimated)**. The repository asserts
**`Gripper.YAW_TOL = 0.5°` (measured)**, and the mount phase is where that
bites: the truss's rods are located by the cage's V-notches, but **the mount
has no V's** — the keeper welds each rod where the tool left it, so gripper
yaw error lands directly in the nose. The mount currently builds to **0.79 mm
and 0.2° (measured, `rig_mount`)** with an ideal servo. An MG996R's backlash
alone is 2–4× the assumed tolerance. A closed-loop alternative — a small
stepper with a reduction, or a smart servo with position feedback — is
warranted for this one axis.

### A4. Smaller corrections

|---|---|---|---|
| A4.1 | Z travel | 300–500 mm rail and screw | `Gantry.Z_TRAVEL = 150` **(measured)**. Buying 500 mm is not harmful but means the Z screw is 3× longer than its stroke, which costs whip, height and money |
| A4.2 | payload camera | Qty **1** RPi Camera Module 2 | a **stereo** rig needs **2** — one per nose. `Payload` is Module 2 and its FOV 62.2 × 48.8° matches the IMX219 exactly **(measured)**, so the part is right and the quantity is not |
| A4.3 | couplings | Qty 3 flexible couplings | there are **4** NEMA 17s (X, Y, Z, dispenser). The dispenser plunger needs its own coupling or leadscrew |

| A4.6 | step resolution | "without microstepping", 200 steps/rev, 4 mm pitch | 0.02 mm/step, matching `Gantry.MM_PER_STEP` **(measured)** — correct and worth keeping, because it is why the machine needs no encoder on X/Y |

Cheaper and more reliable than tapping 50 mm of steel |
| A4.9 | units | inches and millimetres mixed throughout | the entire repository is millimetres. One unit system, and it should be mm |

### A5. What the BOM does not contain at all

Nothing below is optional, and together it is probably a third of the build
cost and most of the schedule risk.

**Electronics — completely absent.** The machine has 4 × NEMA 17, 1 × NEMA 8,
one servo, one DC worm motor, a vacuum pump and a hot wire. That needs:
  * 5 stepper drivers (the NEMA 17s at 2.5 A want a real driver, not an A4988)
  * a 24 V PSU sized for five steppers plus the auxiliaries
  * a motor driver for the worm gear and a MOSFET for the nichrome
  * a controller — the plan is generated and executed as a *program*, not
    G-code, so an SBC (a Pi) with a motion coprocessor, or a microcontroller
    running the executor, has to be chosen. This is an architectural decision
    the document has not made.

**Homing and safety — absent.** Two limit switches per axis minimum (the
repository's whole coordinate system assumes a datum), plus an E-stop that
drops motor power. Without homing there is no relationship between the
machine and the truss frame at all.

**The collapsing mandrel — absent.** This is the single most complex
fabricated assembly in the machine and it is not in the BOM. It is **six
torsion shafts, six over-centre knee braces, 39 rail segments, parallelogram
arms, one draw rod, 96 V-pins, 54 cradles, 6 thread posts and 2 end plates**
**(measured, `check_collapse`)**. It is what makes the truss removable —
collapsed it measures 20.04 mm against a 21.54 mm exit hole, and the release
is one 6.00 mm pull at 0.6 N per knee **(measured)**. It needs its own BOM
section and probably its own drawing set.

**The magazine — absent.** V-blocks under every rod, the two printed camera
nests, the bench plates.

**Station B — absent.** The sub-assembly station that makes the head kit
(module + collar + wound tic-tac-toe) is a second machine with its own jig,
vacuum tool and winder. The main cell cannot wind those four crossings, so
without station B there is no camera mount.

**Consumables — absent.** Per one 1 m truss **(measured)**:

| consumable | quantity per truss |
|---|---|
| 3.0 mm pultruded carbon rod | 3.00 m (3 chords) |
| 1.5 mm pultruded carbon rod | 4.83 m (27 diagonals at 129.4 mm, plus 1.34 m of mount rods) |
| Kevlar thread, 0.15 mm | 5.73 m over 30 joints, 18 turns each |
| two-part epoxy | 5.5 g (4.35 g on the joints, 1.13 g in 36 mount fillets) |
| laser-cut aluminium collar | 2 off, 2.49 g each |
| printed camera carrier | 2 off, 2.0 g each |

Buy rod in 1 m lengths and cut, and note the chosen truss is **30 joints and
27 diagonals**, not the 9 and 24 of the 300 mm truss the simulation demos run
by default.

## PART B — THE DIAGONAL LAYOUT

The insight is correct and the arithmetic works, but not for the reason it
first appears to, and it is tighter than it looks.

### B1. The arithmetic

A gantry with usable stroke *S* on each of two orthogonal axes reaches a
square of side *S*. Put the truss on that square's diagonal and the reach
along the truss becomes *S*√2.

The trap is that *S* is not 1000 mm. On an SFU1204-1000 with BK10/BF10
supports and a DSG12H nut bracket **(estimated — confirm against the parts)**:

    screw blank                              1000 mm
    less the Ø8 journals machined for BK/BF    -50
    less the ball nut's own length             -40
    less end-of-travel safety                  -20
    usable stroke                          ≈  890 mm

The MGN12H rail is not the binding part: a 1000 mm rail with a 45 mm H-block
gives ≈ 955 mm of carriage-centre travel. So:

| usable stroke per axis | diagonal reach |
|---|---|
| 1000 mm (nominal, wrong) | 1414 mm |
| 900 mm | 1273 mm |
| **880 mm (work to this)** | **1245 mm** |
| 850 mm | 1202 mm |

### B2. What has to fit, and the three things that free it

Along the truss axis, the cell must reach **(all measured)**:

| configuration | span needed | fits in 1245? |
|---|---|---|
| as the cell is built today | **1397 mm** | no, short by 152 |
| diagonal racks also moved beside the cage | **1246 mm** | by 1 mm — not a design |
| thread posts + gripper body + tool offset only | **1049 mm** | yes, 196 mm spare |

Three levers, in order of how much they give:

**B2.1 Move the diagonal racks off the ends — 151 mm.** The 1397 is not the
truss; it is 1000 mm of truss plus ~199 mm of diagonal rack beyond *each* end.
The mount's magazine was moved beside the cage for exactly this reason and it
now costs zero X travel; the 27 diagonals are 129 mm long, and 27 of them laid
along the axis in rows of ~11 mm pitch is four rows and 44 mm of cross room.
The end racks exist because the original reasoning considered only diagonals
lying *across* the axis, which would have wanted a 500 mm Y axis. Lying
*along* it they fit beside the cage like the chords.

**B2.2 The brief wants a 1 m BASELINE, not a 1 m truss — 78 mm.** The camera's
optical centre stands **19.55 mm (measured, `mount.fov_standoff`)** proud of
the chord end it is mounted on, at both ends. So

    a 1000 mm truss gives a 1039.1 mm baseline
    a 1000.0 mm baseline needs a truss of only 960.9 mm

That is 39 mm off each end of the span, free, and it is the *stereo* number
that matters — the baseline is what sets depth resolution, not the tube it is
carried on. This should be checked against the brief's wording before it is
banked, but if the brief says baseline, it is the cheapest 78 mm available.

**B2.3 Re-derive `Magazine.CLEAR` — ~20 mm of cross room.** The chord rack
stands off the cage's swept radius by a hand-set 40 mm. What it actually has
to clear is the head's envelope when the head is working the outermost chord,
which is smaller. This matters in B3.

### B3. The reachable region in truss coordinates is a DIAMOND, not a rectangle

This is the part that is easy to miss and would be expensive to find on the
machine. Write *u* for distance along the truss and *v* across it. With the
truss on the diagonal of the reachable square,

    x = (u − v)/√2 ,  y = (u + v)/√2 ,  both in [0, S]
    ⟹  |v| ≤ min( u , S√2 − u )

The cross room **tapers to nothing at both ends of the truss** and is widest
at mid-span. For S = 880 and a 1049 mm span centred on the diagonal:

| where | cross room available |
|---|---|
| at the truss's two ends | **±98 mm** |
| at mid-span | ±622 mm |

Now put the cell's actual racks against that **(all measured)**:

| what | how far off the axis | where it sits | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| the cage itself | ±56 mm | everywhere | fits |
| chord rack (1 m rods, parallel to the axis) | 96 → 120 mm | full length | **±122 mm allowed at S=880 — fits by 2 mm** |
| mount rods | up to 114 mm | **at the noses** | **does not fit: only ±98 mm there** |
| the two camera kits | 119.5 mm | **at the noses** | **does not fit** |

So the mount's magazine — which was just optimised to sit as near each nose
as possible, because that halves the fetch travel — is in the **worst**
possible place for a diagonal machine. It is at the ends, which is precisely
where the diamond pinches.

That is not an argument against the diagonal layout. It is an argument that
**the rule the magazine packs by has to change**: "the free station nearest
the nose it is laid at" is correct for a rectangular envelope and wrong for a
diamond. It becomes "the nearest **reachable** station", with reachability
asked of the machine's real envelope rather than of two independent intervals.
On a diagonal machine the mount parts move toward mid-span and the fetch gets
longer — the 2112 mm of fetch travel currently measured would rise, and that
is the right trade.

### B4. Mechanical consequences of the 45° mount

* **The head rotates 45° about Z on the carriage.** The ring's plane must stay
  perpendicular to the chord, the gripper's yaw datum must be the truss axis,
  and the tool offsets (`grip_x`, `disp_x`) are along the truss. Mounting the
  whole head at a fixed 45° costs nothing and avoids adding a rotary axis.
* **The frame must be bigger than the rails.** The BK10/BF10 blocks, couplings
  and motors need ≈ 120 mm beyond each rail end **(estimated)**, so a
  1000 mm-rail machine has an outside dimension nearer 1240 mm. The document's
  "1000 mm × 1000 mm square perimeter" is the rail envelope, not the frame.
* **The cage lies corner to corner.** The fixture is **1090 mm overall
  (measured: 1000 mm of chord plus 45 mm of end freedom and end plate at each
  end)** in a 1245 mm diagonal, so centred it leaves **77 mm at each end** —
  which is exactly where the ballscrew end blocks and the frame's corner
  gussets are. The corners must be cut away, or the fixture carried on
  outriggers past them. At S = 900 it is 91 mm, which is still not enough for
  a BK10 plus a coupling plus a NEMA 17.
* **Squareness stops being cosmetic and becomes the dominant error.** Today
  the truss axis *is* the X rail, so its straightness is the rail's. On a
  diagonal it is a virtual axis, and an out-of-square ε between X and Y bows
  it by about *u*·ε/2: a typical unshimmed 0.001 rad over 1000 mm is **0.5 mm
  of bow (estimated)**. The truss itself does not care — the V-pins hold it —
  but the mount has no V's, so that 0.5 mm lands straight on the nose, which
  currently builds to 0.79 mm. **Squareness must be measured and compensated
  as a shear term in software, not shimmed and hoped for.**
* **Feed rate and resolution.** A truss-axis move drives both axes, so it is
  limited by the slower: 40 mm/s each gives 56.6 mm/s resultant against the
  60 mm/s X alone today **(measured `Gantry.V_MAX`)**. Step resolution along
  the axis becomes 0.0283 mm and the cross-axis quantisation ±0.0141 mm — both
  inside the band tolerance, but the path is a staircase rather than a line.

## PART C — WHAT WOULD HAVE TO CHANGE IN THE SIMULATION

High-level specification only. In rough dependency order.

**C1. A machine frame distinct from the truss frame, and exactly one
transform between them.** The plan stays in truss coordinates (u, v, z); the
HAL maps to machine (x, y, z). The seam belongs at `process._move` /
`cell.CellSim.goto`, because everything above it already speaks truss
coordinates and nothing below it needs to. The layout — axis directions,
origin, and the *measured* squareness shear — becomes data on `spec.Gantry`,
so a 0° layout and a 45° layout are the same code with different data. This
is the whole change, if it is done once and in one place.

**C2. `Gantry.X_TRAVEL` / `Y_TRAVEL` become per-axis machine strokes.** 1400
and 300 stop meaning anything. They become ≈ 880 and ≈ 880, and the layout
angle is what turns those into reach along the truss.

**C3. Replace `Fixture.x_range()` and `y_reach()` with one reachability
test.** This is the important one. Two independent intervals cannot express a
diamond: a station can be inside both and still unreachable. The test should
take every point the cell must actually visit — rack slots and their whole
footprints, thread posts, joint stations, mount release poses, bond points,
the head's home — map each through the transform, and assert the machine
coordinates are inside the axes' strokes. It subsumes both old checks and it
is what would catch a part racked in a pinched corner.

**C4. The magazine solver takes reachability as its region.** `_mount_rack`
already packs against a span with per-flank floors; it would instead ask the
C3 predicate. Its ordering rule changes from "nearest the nose it is laid at"
to "nearest reachable station", as B3 explains.

**C5. Move the diagonal racks beside the cage.** Independently worthwhile —
it is the 151 mm of B2.1 and it makes the layout feasible. The same pattern
the mount rack now uses: rows along the axis on whichever flank is free.

**C6. MJCF: give the carriage two prismatic joints at ±45° and leave the rest
of the scene in truss coordinates.** The cheap route by a wide margin. Every
existing geometric check — the cage, the racks, the clearances, the built-scene
measurements — stays valid unchanged, and only the joint axes, their ranges and
the head body's 45° rotation move. Re-laying the whole scene in machine
coordinates would invalidate all of it for no gain.

**C7. Planned durations change.** `move_time` already coordinates multi-axis
trapezoids, so the form is unchanged, but a truss-axis move is now bounded by
the slower axis. Phase times in `schedule.summary` will shift and the
"overran the plan" tolerances want re-checking against them.

**C8. Squareness becomes a calibration constant with a rig behind it.** The
project's own rule: a constant with a suite that re-measures it is a
measurement, and the same constant without one is a guess. The shear term
belongs beside `M2.HOPPER_SPREAD` and the pace factor — measured by a rig,
asserted by a check, so it cannot drift away from the machine.

**C9. Re-run the design sweep.** `structure.py` rejects truss candidates on
`x_reach > X_TRAVEL` and `y_reach > Y_TRAVEL/2`. Under C3 that becomes one
diamond test, which changes **which trusses are buildable at all** — so
`spine/chosen.py` has to be re-derived, and the chosen section may move. Do
this last, and expect the answer to change.

**C10. The truss length becomes an output of the baseline, not an input.** If
B2.2 holds, the spec should carry the *baseline* as the requirement and derive
the truss length as `baseline − 2 × standoff`. That keeps the 39 mm per end
from being quietly lost the next time the standoff changes.

---

## PART D — WHAT TO MEASURE BEFORE COMMITTING TO ANY OF IT

Cheap, and each one settles a number the rest depends on.

1. **Usable stroke of one axis, as assembled.** Mount one screw with its
   BK10/BF10 and nut bracket and measure the travel between hard stops. Every
   number in Part B scales off it. If it comes in under 850 mm the diagonal
   layout needs B2.2 as well as B2.1, not instead of it.
2. **Squareness of the two axes.** A dial indicator and a granite square, or
   the diagonal-difference method on the frame. This is the input to C8 and
   the only thing that decides whether the mount phase survives the layout.
3. **The Y bridge's first mode.** Tap it with the Z assembly mounted and watch
   the decay on any accelerometer, including a phone's. If it is near 11 Hz,
   A2 is confirmed before any rail is drilled.
4. **The cage indexer's repeatability.** Index to the same station twenty
   times from alternating directions and measure with an indicator on the end
   plate. Against 0.05° this will either pass or fail unambiguously, and it
   decides A3's detent question.
5. **The gripper servo's backlash.** Command ±90° repeatedly and measure the
   return angle. The mount's 0.79 mm depends on this one number more than on
   anything else in the machine.

## PART E — DECISION LOG

*Decisions taken and closed, with the reasoning that closed them. A decision
recorded here is settled; the alternatives are written down so that the next
person to have the same idea can see it was already costed rather than
overlooked.*

### E1 — 2026-09-15 · Z axis stays on a profile rail and a ball screw

**The question.** The Z axis does not carry much load. Could it use a lead
screw and smooth rods instead of an SFU1204 ball screw and an MGN12H profile
rail, as a saving?

**What the machine actually asks of Z (measured).**

| | |
|---|---|
| Z stroke | 150 mm (`Gantry.Z_TRAVEL`) — not the 300–500 mm the BOM buys, see A4.1 |
| Z duty per 1 m truss | 381 moves, 4628 mm of travel, **116 s of a 35.8 min plan — 5 %** |
| tool offset from the column | **30.5 mm** (nozzle) and **15 mm** (grip point) |
| dispenser standoff | **1.0 mm** — the tightest Z-dependent tolerance in the machine |
| step resolution | 0.020 mm, and `Gantry.MM_PER_STEP` is **one value for all three axes** |

Two things came out of asking the question that are worth keeping whatever
was decided:

* **Z is a coarse positioner, not a fine one.** The gripper and the
  dispenser each have their own 40 mm stroke (`gg`, `gd`) and it is that
  stroke, not Z, which makes the final approach to the work. The premise
  behind the question was sound, and this is the reason for it.
* **The load on Z is a moment, not a weight.** With the tools standing 15 to
  30 mm off the column, and the yaw servo's reaction torque — of order
  1.1 N·m at an MG996R's stall **(estimated)** against roughly 0.4 N·m of
  self-weight moment — reacting straight into the Z carriage, the largest
  thing Z resists is torque rather than mass.

**The lead-screw alternative, as costed.** It was not a bad option. A lead
screw can be self-locking where a ball screw cannot, and Z's tiny duty means
a fine lead costs almost nothing in time:

| lead | mm/step | rpm at 40 mm/s | lead angle | self-locking? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 mm | 0.010 | 1200 | 5.2° | yes |
| 4 mm | 0.020 | 600 | 10.3° | borderline |
| 8 mm (the 3D-printer default) | 0.040 | 300 | 20.0° | **no** |

Self-locking needs the lead angle under the friction angle — 5.7° at μ = 0.10,
11.3° at μ = 0.20 for steel on POM **(estimated)**. Tr8×2 at 20 mm/s would
have given 600 rpm, self-locking, and 0.01 mm/step, for about two minutes
added to a 36-minute build.

**The decision: keep the MGN12H rail and the SFU1204 ball screw on Z.**

What it buys:

* **One part family across all three axes.** The same BK10/BF10 supports,
  DSG12H nut housing, coupling and spares serve X, Y and Z. Three of a thing
  is easier to buy, align and replace than two of one and one of another.
* **Backlash stops being a question.** A preloaded ball nut closes the one
  real casualty the lead screw would have had: `dose` is the single
  accuracy-critical op that approaches its height from *above* — 0 down, 30
  up, measured — and the nozzle standoff is 1.0 mm, on a machine that has
  already recorded that a tip one millimetre out "spawned the drop inside the
  chord and the solver threw it to the floor: seven of twelve". With a ball
  screw that concern does not arise. (Every rod `release` — 50 of 50 — already
  descends, so the mount's 0.79 mm was never exposed to it either way.)
* **Angular stiffness where the loading is angular.** The moment loading above
  is what a preloaded profile-rail block is for, and what round rods with ball
  bushings are worst at. The objection to rods was never deflection — two
  LM8UU 50 mm apart give about 0.04 mm at the tool **(estimated)**, which is
  inside tolerance — it was **hysteresis**: deflection is repeatable and can be
  mapped, play cannot, and the mount phase has no V-notches to filter it.
* **No spec change.** `MM_PER_STEP` stays one value for three axes.

What it costs, and this is now a live requirement rather than a footnote:

* **A ball screw is backdrivable, so the head falls when the power is off.**
  At roughly 90 % efficiency there is nothing holding the Z carriage but the
  motor. The build needs one of: holding current on the Z stepper (and the
  heat and standby power that implies), a fail-safe brake, or a
  counterbalance. **This must be added to the electronics list in A5, which
  does not yet have it** — along with the behaviour on E-stop, where cutting
  motor power drops the head onto the work.

**Still open, and not affected by this decision:** the Z travel discrepancy
(A4.1 — 150 mm used against a 300–500 mm rail and screw specified), and the
two actuators the BOM does not carry. The cell models **nine** — X, Y, Z,
dispenser stroke, gripper stroke, gripper yaw, jaws, ring, cage — and the BOM
specifies **seven**. The gripper's 40 mm stroke and the jaw actuator are
missing.

---

### E2 — 2026-09-16 · Z reverts to a self-locking lead screw

**Decision: Z becomes a Tr8×2 lead screw. X and Y keep their SFU1204 ball
screws.** This supersedes E1 for the Z axis only.

**Why E1 is no longer the right answer.** E1 kept the ball screw on four
legs, and PART F voided one of them: *"`dose` is the single accuracy-critical
op that approaches its height from **above** — 0 down, 30 up, measured."*
With resin dosing off the machine, **every remaining Z op descends** — a rod
`release` descends (50 of 50, measured by E1), and the ring descends onto
the chord. Backlash taken up in one direction does not appear.

**What the lead screw buys, and it is the thing E1 had to carry as a cost.**
E1 recorded backdriving as *"a live requirement rather than a footnote"*:
a ball screw at ~90 % efficiency holds nothing when the power is off, so the
build needed one of a holding current, a fail-safe brake, or a
counterbalance — plus an E-stop hazard where cutting motor power drops the
head onto the work. A Tr8×2 is **self-locking**: 5.2° of lead angle against
a 5.7° friction angle at μ = 0.10 **(class value, not measured)**. The
requirement disappears, the brake disappears, and the E-stop stops being a
hazard.

Tr8×**4** would have needed no spec change at all — 4 mm / 200 steps is
exactly the 0.0200 mm/step the ball screw gives — but its 10.3° lead angle is
the "borderline" row in E1's own table, so the self-locking benefit, which is
the whole reason for the change, would have been a coin toss.

| | before (SFU1204) | after (Tr8×2) |
|---|---|---|
| mm per full step, Z | 0.0200 | **0.0100** |
| self-locking | no | **yes** |
| `V_MAX["z"]` | 40 mm/s | **20 mm/s** |
| screw rpm at `V_MAX` | 600 | 600 |
| metre-truss plan | 26.46 min | **29.15 min** |

**What it costs, measured rather than estimated.** At 40 mm/s a 2 mm lead
wants 1200 rpm, which whips and wears a POM nut, so `V_MAX["z"]` comes down
to 20. **That costs 162 s on the metre truss — 10.2 % of a 26.5 minute
build.** E1's estimate was "about two minutes"; it is 2.7.

Also lost: E1's "one part family across three axes". Two families now.

**Code landed with this decision:** `Gantry.PITCH_Z = 2.0`,
`Gantry.pitch(axis)` and `Gantry.mm_per_step(axis)`; `cell._quant` and both
`check_hal` step assertions are per-axis; `spec.CHECKS` now asserts a full
step is finer than `REPEAT` on **every** axis rather than on one.

---

### E3 — 2026-09-16 · The machine's own vision is deferred to an external rig

**Decision: no camera is designed into the head.** An existing stereo rig is
placed in the workspace, and perception is deferred.

`Vision` exists for one job: *"find the chord's actual y and the mitre line's
x before lowering, so the fixture's ±1 mm becomes a non-issue."* Deferring it
makes that ±1 mm live again, so the question is what the approach margin can
absorb. **Measured** — the head put at the planned pose with the rod
somewhere else, over all 30 joints of the metre truss:

| head off by | +y / −y | +z | −z | +x | worst |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 mm | 1.91 | 1.91 | 1.91 | 1.91 | **1.91** |
| 0.25 | 1.69 | 1.74 | 1.94 | 1.76 | 1.69 |
| 0.50 | 1.45 | 1.58 | 1.92 | 1.63 | 1.45 |
| 1.00 | 0.95 | 1.29 | 1.94 | 1.27 | **0.95** |
| 1.50 | 0.46 | 0.90 | 1.92 | 0.96 | **0.46** |

**Nothing collides out to 1.5 mm**, but the margin is spent: `±y` is the
worst direction and costs about 0.96 mm of clearance per mm of error, and
anything past **0.45 mm** eats into the 1.5 mm the plan reserves as
`Process.SEAT_CLEAR`.

**The mitigation is free and it is in the manual process, not the machine.**
`Stock.CUT_TOL = 0.3 mm` (1σ) is the hand-cut rod-length scatter — 0.9 mm at
3σ, which lands on the 0.95 mm row. **Cut the rods against a length stop**
and 3σ falls to about 0.3 mm, which keeps the worst case near 1.6 mm, above
the reserve. A saw stop costs nothing and removes the reason for the camera.

**So: no head camera, and cut the rods on a stop.** If a build shows the
band landing off-centre, vision comes back — the model, the render check and
`check_vision` are all still in the tree.

## PART F — THE DIVISION OF LABOUR

The objective of the project is to prove that a **scalable process** exists
for building the stereo camera's spine. That makes the design question not
"what can be done cheaply" but:

> **Which operations must a machine do, because a person cannot do them at
> all at the precision required — and which can a person keep doing
> forever without the process stopping being scalable?**

This part answers that, and the answer removes four of the cell's nine
actuators. It was written after a question exposed that the winding
*process* had never been described anywhere: how the thread gets from the
ring to the rod, how it starts, how it stops. The simulation planned the
motion in detail and the mechanism was a duration with a name.

---

### F1. The rule, and the answer it gives

A manual step does not stop a process being scalable when it is either:

- **not O(joints)** — its count is set by the number of chords or rods, so
  it does not grow as the truss does; or
- **precision-free** — something other than the human hand holds the
  tolerance.

Everything that is **O(joints) *and* precision-critical** is the machine's.
On the metre truss that divides as follows:

| operation | count | grows with | what holds the tolerance | who |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| load a thread cartridge | **3** | chords | nothing — a bench jig | **hand** |
| anchor: loop the strand round the post | **3** | chords | the first hoop traps it | **hand** |
| cut at the far post | **3** | chords | nothing | **hand** |
| index the cage between chords | **6** | chords | a detent or an index pin | **hand** or machine |
| **place a rod in the fixture** | **30** | **rods = joints** | the fixture's V-notch, but see below | **MACHINE** |
| **position the ring at a joint** | **30** | **joints** | **the axes, to a 1.94 mm margin** | **MACHINE** |
| **wind 18 turns with the axial feed** | **30** | **joints** | **0.444 mm per turn** | **MACHINE** |
| dose 145 mg of resin | 30 | joints | a syringe with a stop, ±7% on a 0.001 g scale | hand, for now |

Two things in that table matter more than the rest.

**Rod loading was put in the hand column on a wrong count, and it moves
back.** The first draft of this table said nine rods, which is the **300 mm**
truss's figure; the metre truss is **3 chords + 27 diagonals = 30 rods**, one
per joint. That breaks the O(joints) test outright: rod placement grows
exactly as fast as the winding does.

The precision argument survives — `fixture.py` says *"GEOMETRY IS HELD BY
THE FIXTURE (brief 4.1)"*, and a rod dropped into its V-notch lands where a
gripper would have put it — but it is not enough on its own. A diagonal goes
into **two** cradles at a computed roll angle with a mitred end on each side
(`theta_face_up`, "a diagonal drops into its two cradles when its face is
uppermost and level"), twenty-seven times. That is the "precision work I
cannot manually do even if I had the time".

**So the gripper stays, and the cell is EIGHT actuators, not five:** X, Y,
Z, ring, cage, gripper stroke, gripper yaw, jaws. Only the dispenser comes
off. (PART H later adds a ninth — a fine **yaw** stage — and what that one
buys is the deletion of the 1400 mm rail; see H3.)

**The glue is the one weak link in the scalability claim.** It is O(joints)
— 30 doses, and 300 on a ten-truss run. It is also the only manual step
that grows, which makes it the first thing to automate later, and the
dispenser is already designed and measured (`check_effectors`). It stays
manual now because it is precision-light, not because it is scalable.

---

### F2. What the winding mechanism is, and what the groove is for

One ring revolution lays one hoop. The thread's far end is anchored, the
ring's exit guide orbits the rod, and each orbit wraps one turn. It is a
lathe with the spindle and the tool exchanged.

The groove exists for exactly one reason, and it is **not** storage:

> **The supply must orbit with the guide.** A bobbin standing still while
> the guide orbits would have the strand between them twist up and wind onto
> the *bobbin* instead of the rod.

Everything else about the groove — the 2 × 2 mm section, `PACKING = 0.6`,
the capacity figure — is *packaging*. It keeps the head's swept radius at
the rim (20 mm instead of the old rim-mounted spool's 32 mm), which is what
let `Cage` grow its spine from 3 mm to 8 mm.

A bobbin bolted to the ring's face would also orbit, and the obvious
objection to a lopsided wheel does not survive arithmetic:

| bobbin | radius | speed | out of balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 g | 15 mm | 60 rpm (`Ring.RPM`) | 0.0012 N |
| 5 g | 15 mm | 150 rpm (`RPM_MAX`) | 0.0185 N |

against a winding tension of 2.0 N — 100× to 1700× smaller than the force
it is fighting. Balance is not a constraint at these speeds. **But envelope
still is**, which is why F5 keeps the supply inboard of the rim rather than
bolting it on.

---

### F3. The anchor and the cut are three per truss, not thirty

This was already in `schedule.py` and had not been read: *"Chord runs
alternate direction. The thread is continuous along a chord."*

The plan loops four legs round a **thread post on the fixture**
(`Process.ANCHOR_LEGS = 4`, `Cage.POST_R`, `Cage.POST_L`, and the `post_off`
window solved earlier), runs the thread continuously along the chord through
all ten of its joints — carrying 97.7 mm between bands — and cuts once at
the far post. Then it reverses for the next chord.

| | metre truss |
|---|---|
| joints / chords | 30 on 3, ten each |
| thread per joint | 191 mm |
| thread per **chord run** | 2718 mm |
| thread, whole truss | 8.16 m, 0.208 g |
| **anchors** | **3** |
| **cuts** | **3** |

The rod is never touched to anchor. The thread is never cut mid-chord.
**Three of each is O(chords), so both belong in the hand column
permanently — not as a prototype compromise.**

---

### F4. What that deletes from the design

Hand-cutting removes a real material problem rather than deferring it.
`Cutter` is specified as a hot wire on the head (`CUT_S = 1.0`,
`WIRE_OFF = 4.0`):

| thread | hot wire | hand clippers |
|---|---|---|
| nylon | works — melts ~220 °C (nylon 6) to ~260 °C (6,6) and fuses the end | fine |
| para-aramid (Kevlar) | **chars** — no melting point, decomposes ~430–480 °C, so the end frays | fine |
| E-glass | **will not part it** — softens ~840 °C, above a small nichrome element | fine |

**(Softening and decomposition figures are class values, not measured.)**
A hot wire suits the shakedown thread and neither material the truss would
actually ship with. A pair of clippers suits all three, and is used three
times per truss.

What comes off the machine, in total:

| off the machine | actuators freed | what stops being needed |
|---|---|---|
| resin dosing | `a_d` dispenser stroke | the `dose` op, `DOSE_SETTLE_S`, the drop-placement model, the interleaved-vs-separate-pass comparison |
| the cut | — (it was a hot wire, not an axis) | `Cutter` entirely, and its material problem |
| the anchor | — | `Process.ANCHOR_S`, `ANCHOR_LEGS`, and `approach.post_loop` — the solved rectangle the head ran round the post, six moves per chord, which had a recorded failure: the hand-drawn version *"hooked the ring on the post, jammed the x axis, and put the first look 60 mm from its joint"* |
| loading the spool | — | any machine-loadable groove; the target becomes **hand**-loadable (F5) |

**The cell goes from nine actuators to eight**: the dispenser is the only
one removed, because rod placement is O(joints) and moves back to the
machine (F1). The gripper, its yaw, its jaws and the magazine all stay.
PART H then spends the freed slot on a fine yaw stage, so the count returns
to nine — a *different* nine, and the 1400 mm rail goes instead.

**A knock-on to PART E that must not rot.** E1's case for keeping a ball
screw on Z listed four reasons, and one of them was: *"`dose` is the single
accuracy-critical op that approaches its height from above."* With the
dispenser off the machine, **that reason is void.** The other three — one
part family across three axes, angular stiffness where the loading is
angular, and no spec change — stand, as does the backdriving cost. The
decision does not reverse; one of its legs is gone.

---

### F5. How a hand loads the thread, three times

The requirement changes from "a machine must load the groove" to "a person
must load it in under a minute, three times per truss, without dismantling
the drive." That is a much easier target, and it makes the **cartridge** the
right answer rather than a nicety.

**The lay is shallow, which is the fact that makes this work.** Ask not
"how much fits at 60% packing" — the question nobody needed answered, whose
answer (10.67 m, 136 channel-lengths coiled into 79 mm) is what made the
groove look unworkable — but **how much fits in a single flat layer that
cannot nest on itself**:

| | 0.15 mm Kevlar | 0.11 mm nylon |
|---|---|---|
| strands abreast in the 2 mm channel | 13 | 18 |
| **one flat layer** | **1.05 m** | **1.43 m** |
| one chord run, 2.72 m | 2.6 layers = **0.39 mm** | 1.9 layers = **0.21 mm** |
| a whole truss, 8.16 m | 7.8 layers = 1.17 mm | 5.7 layers = 0.63 mm |
| the channel's depth | 2.0 mm | 2.0 mm |

One chord's thread occupies **under a fifth of the channel's depth** in two
or three shallow layers. The snarl risk was self-inflicted by a capacity
figure.

**This relaxes a stated intent, so it is recorded rather than assumed.**
`spec.CHECKS` asserts *"...and holds a whole truss of thread, so nothing is
reloaded mid-cycle"* (`groove_capacity(0.15) > 8.0`). The assertion still
passes — the channel *can* hold 10.67 m. What F5 proposes is not using that
capacity.

**The cartridge, as a proposal (not yet measured or drawn):**

- a thin C-shaped shell that carries the channel, keyed to the ring's gap by
  one pin and pushed on **axially from the work side** — the rail covers only
  r 18.2 to 30, so the ring's face inboard of that is open, and a cartridge
  living at r 12 to 18 can go on and off with the ring still captive in its
  rail;
- **a lip or peg at each end of the channel.** This is the retention problem
  the bare groove has and does not solve: a channel open on one face that
  simply stops at ±30° has nothing stopping the lay sliding out
  circumferentially. A cartridge can have end stops because it clears
  nothing;
- the free end pokes out of a hole near the bore, for the hand to thread
  through the exit guide and loop round the post.

**And the bench jig that loads it is trivial.** One revolution of the
cartridge lays one 79 mm circuit, so 2.72 m is **34 revolutions** — about
35 seconds at 60 rpm off a cordless drill. The traverse across a 2 mm
channel over 13 revolutions is slow enough to guide with a finger. A plate
with a boss and a crank, three times per truss.

---

### F6. The one thing hand-dosing genuinely changes

It is **not** wet-versus-dry winding. The plan already winds dry and doses
afterwards (`P.add("wind", …)` then `dose(…)`), so hand-dosing moves *when*
the resin arrives, not *whether* the band is wetted after the fact.

What it changes is **how long a dry band must hold its position**: about a
second in the interleaved plan, versus the full winding pass — of order
twenty minutes — if a person doses all thirty afterwards. That makes one
existing question load-bearing that was not before. `check_ring` already
asks it: *"whether the wound band alone holds them once the keeper lets
go."* It now has to hold for twenty minutes across thirty joints rather
than momentarily across one.

The separate dosing pass is already plannable as `interleave=False`, so both
orders can be compared on the clock and, more importantly, on the bench.
**Measure it at P0** (F7): wind a coupon dry, leave it twenty minutes, then
dose, and see whether the band moved and whether the resin still wets
through 193 mm of thread. It is a free column in the DoE and it gates the
whole hand-glue plan.

---

### F7. The purchase order is an uncertainty order, not a budget

The whole cell is in scope. What is staged is the *order*, so that a wrong
assumption is found on the cheapest possible purchase.

**P0 — the lathe. No C-ring at all.** Spin the **work**, not the ring: two
rods in a V-jig on a spindle, thread off a stationary bobbin through a
felt-pad tensioner. This is what `check_ring` already does in simulation and
for this reason: *"a spinning ring with a spool on its rim needs its pay-out
reserve carried round with it… So the rig turns the work instead."*

Why it can never be the machine: a rod already built into a truss is held at
both ends and cannot be spun. **That single fact is the entire justification
for the C-ring**, and it is worth keeping in view, because it is the only
reason any of the ring, rail, pinion and belt complexity exists.

But for a coupon it beats winding by hand, because tension and turn count
become repeatable. It retires the five numbers the whole structural design
rests on and that nobody has measured: `tension = 2.0 [VERIFY by DoE]`,
`turns = 18`, `band = 8.0`, the 145 mg dose, and the 50 N qualification
pull — plus F6's dry-hold question. Cost: a V-jig, a bobbin, a felt washer
and a spring on a motor already owned.

**P1 — the C-ring turning, on a bench.** The parts in `sim/cad/`. This
answers what nothing in simulation can: does the thread catch at the 60°
gap; does the ring wedge in its rail; does a pinion lose a tooth
re-entering; does a hand-loaded lay pay out at a constant tension. Print the
rail in four clearance variants — which of them turns *is* the measurement.

**P2 — one real axis.** The 250–300 mm MGN12 + SFU1605, used as the band
feed. It tests the rail/screw/driver combination all three axes will use, on
the cheapest possible sample. If the band pitch turns out not to matter
(P1 can test that by winding a pile instead of a band), the feed can even be
geared off the ring's own rotation the way a thread-cutting lathe is:
`8.0 / 18 = 0.444 mm per ring revolution`, which on a 2 mm screw is a 4.5:1
belt and no second controller.

**P3 — the rest of the axes, the cage, and the machine.** Nothing here is
in doubt that the simulation has not already measured: `check_approach` puts
the descent at 1.94 mm and the seated pose at 1.91 mm over all 30 joints.

---

### F8. What this part adds to the open list

1. **The spec and the schedule do not yet say any of this.** `spec.CHECKS`
   still asserts that nothing is reloaded mid-cycle; the schedule still
   plans a machine anchor (`post_loop`, `ANCHOR_S`) and a machine cut
   (`Cutter`); the HAL still carries nine actuators. **This is a design
   decision recorded in prose and not yet in code**, and prose is where
   decisions rot. Until it lands, `check_hal`'s actuator contract and
   `check_schedule`'s op list describe a machine nobody intends to build.
2. **The live tail is not modelled as an obstacle anywhere.**
   `approach.head_distance` models the ring's annulus, the raceway's, three
   pinion discs and the head box — not the thread. The MJCF cell does draw
   the wound band, but `contype=0 conaffinity=0`: *"a visual band inside the
   section, adding nothing to the envelope."* So nothing checks that the
   strand from the exit guide to the work is clear of the descent. This is
   the cheapest check left to add — the strand is one capsule from `EXIT_R`
   to the joint — and it is worth more now, because a hand doing the anchor
   means a human is judging that clearance by eye, three times.
3. **The cartridge is a proposal, not a design.** F5 has no drawing, no
   retention measurement and no check. It needs the same treatment the rail
   and pinion got: derived from `spec.Ring`, emitted as an STL, and checked.
4. **The mount phase has not been divided.** F1's table covers building the
   truss. Putting the camera bracket on it is 20 parts placed to 0.80 mm and
   0.2° (measured). Since the gripper stays for rod placement anyway, this
   no longer changes the actuator count — but it does decide whether the
   magazine carries 20 more parts.

## PART G — THE PROCESS, END TO END

The agreed division, as one sequence. **H** is a hand, **M** is the machine.
Counts are for the metre truss (30 joints, 30 rods, 3 chords).

| # | step | who | ×  | already built? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | cut 30 rods to length, **against a stop** (E3) | **H** | 30 | — |
| 2 | lay the rods in the magazine racks | **H** | 30 | `Magazine`, and the rack layout is solved |

**Hands touch it 96 times, and 6 of those are the ones that matter.** Steps
5 and 8 are O(chords) — three loads and three cuts, which do not grow with
the truss. Steps 1, 2 and 9 are O(joints) and precision-light. Steps 4, 6
and 7 are the machine's, and step 4 is there because 27 mitred diagonals into
54 cradles at computed roll angles is not hand work (F1).

### G1. The machine that results

**Nine actuators**, and PART H changed which nine: the dispenser is gone
(F4) and a fine **yaw** stage has taken its place (H3). Z is a lead screw
(E2). No camera on the head — but vision is now load-bearing rather than
deferred, because H reverses E3.

```
GANTRY ARCHITECTURE (what the sim models today)
  X  1400 mm travel, SFU1204 ball screw, 0.0200 mm/step
  Y   300 mm travel, SFU1204 ball screw, 0.0200 mm/step

COARSE-FINE ARCHITECTURE (PART H; not yet in the sim)
  coarse     a mecanum base: get the joint inside reach, nothing more
  X   500 mm screw = 361 mm stroke, 0.0200 mm/step } on the trolley plate,
  Y   500 mm screw = 361 mm stroke, 0.0200 mm/step } nulling the park
  yaw +-10 deg, lever + Tr8x2,   0.0057 deg/step  } from the tags

COMMON TO BOTH
  Z   150 mm travel, Tr8x2 LEAD screw, 0.0100 mm/step, self-locking
  ring       module 0.8, 48T rim, 3 pinions, one belt   (sim/cad/)
  cage       worm-driven, 6 index positions
  gripper    stroke + yaw + jaws
  plan       29.15 min for the metre truss, 891 ops (gantry; H re-prices it)
```

### G2. The base structure folds, and this was the expensive lesson

Step 3 and step 11 are already designed and checked, and it is worth knowing
why they needed designing. `collapse.py`:

> *"The truss is a closed triangular lattice wound onto the cage… the chosen
> 85 mm truss has 96 pins, 54 cradles, 6 posts. The biggest cylinder that can
> slide out along the axis is 21.54 mm. The pins' notch floors reach 46.95
> mm. **The cage was drawn as a thing to wind onto and never as a thing to
> get back**, and no amount of clearance fixes it: the lattice closes around
> it."*

What replaced it is a collapsible mandrel: rails rather than arms, on
**parallelogram** linkages so a V-notch translates instead of rotating (a
notch that rotated as it retreated would rake the rod); the chord rails
**segmented** onto `fixture.free_spans` so the ring can come down between
them, twelve pieces per chord; six **torsion shafts** on the spine's surface
at the arms' own hinge line — the one radius the head can never reach — so
one shaft folds a whole chord's worth; and six **over-centre** toggle locks,
because a toggle taken past dead centre is driven harder into its stop by
the load it carries and cannot be undone by the winding tension.

`check_collapse` has 101 checks and its **first** one asserts that the cage
*as originally drawn* is trapped — so if that check ever passes trivially,
the rest of the suite is measuring nothing.

### G3. What still has to change in code

This part is agreed and the code does not yet say it. In blast-radius order:

1. **Remove the dispenser** — `Dispenser`, `a_d` in the HAL and the MJCF,
   `schedule.dose`, `DOSE_SETTLE_S`, the `dose` op and the
   interleave comparison. Touches `check_hal`'s actuator contract,
   `check_schedule`'s op list, `check_model`, `check_cycle`. **Keep**
   `band.resin_dose_mg` and `drop_radius_mm`: a hand still needs to know it
   is 145 mg, and the dispenser may come back (F1 — the glue is the one
   manual step that is O(joints)).
2. **Remove the cutter and the machine anchor** — `Cutter`, `ANCHOR_S`,
   `ANCHOR_LEGS`, `approach.post_loop`, and the six moves per chord the plan
   spends running the head round the post. **Keep** `Cage.POST_R/POST_L` and
   the `post_off` window: the post is still what the thread anchors to, a
   hand just ties it.
3. **Remove the `look`** — `Vision`, `look_x`, `check_vision`, and 30 look
   ops. Band start becomes `band_start` directly rather than a look followed
   by a move (E3).
4. **Design the cartridge** (F5) and add a load stop to the plan: the machine
   pauses at each chord's start for a hand.
5. **Relax the reload assertion** — `spec.CHECKS` still says the groove
   *"holds a whole truss of thread, so nothing is reloaded mid-cycle"*.
   It now reloads three times, by hand, on purpose.

Items 1 to 3 all *remove* things, which is the safest kind of change and the
kind these suites are best at catching. None of them is done yet.

## PART H — COARSE-FINE POSITIONING ON A MOBILE BASE

**The proposal.** Drop the long precise axis. A mecanum-wheeled robot
(already owned) carries a trolley until the target joint lies somewhere in
the fine stages' reach — 361 x 361 mm on 500 mm screws (I2), and how far
off it is does not matter;
two **250 mm** rail-and-ball-screw stages on the trolley plate take it the
rest of the way;
a stereo rig on a 250 mm baseline (already owned) closes the loop; Z stays
the Tr8×2 lead screw of E2. Later the syringe and, if ever wanted, a cutter
ride the same Z.

**This is a standard architecture, not a compromise.** The 1400 mm X axis is
expensive *because it has to be both long and precise*, and those two
requirements are separable — which is what every wafer stepper, large-format
machining centre and mobile manipulator does. Splitting them is the textbook
answer, and here it lands on hardware that is already on the shelf.

---

### H1. It makes PART B unnecessary, and that is the biggest consequence

PART B exists for one reason: **1 m is the procurement limit for rails and
ball screws, and the metre truss needs 1397 mm of X.** Everything in it —
the 880 mm usable window opened to a 1245 mm diagonal, the 1397/1246/1049
span arithmetic, the 960.9 mm truss that yields a 1000.0 mm baseline, the
**diamond**-shaped reachable region, and the C1–C10 list of simulation
changes — is a workaround for that one constraint.

**A mobile coarse axis removes the constraint.** X travel stops being bounded
by what can be bought, so the truss can lie square to the machine and the
diagonal layout, the diamond and C1–C10 can all be dropped. That is worth
settling *before* anything is ordered, because it changes which truss the
sim should be solving for.

---

### H2. Two different budgets, belonging to two different parts

Two measurements against `approach`, over all 30 joints of the metre truss.
The plan's reserve is `Process.SEAT_CLEAR = 1.5 mm` and its clearance with
everything perfect is **1.91 mm**. **The two tables below are not two views
of one requirement — they belong to different hardware**, and the first
draft of this part ran them together and got the architecture wrong as a
result.

**The TRANSLATION budget is the FINE STAGES' and the camera's**, not the
trolley's. It is the residual after the stages have moved, so it measures
how well vision plus a ball screw can place the ring — not how well the
wheels arrived. The worst direction is ±y and it costs about **0.96 mm of
clearance per mm of residual**. Past **0.45 mm** it eats the reserve;
nothing contacts out to 1.5 mm. Against `REPEAT = 0.05 mm` and a camera good
to ~0.05 mm (H4), there is an order of magnitude of headroom.

**The ROTATION budget is the TROLLEY'S**, and it is the only one that is,
because nothing on the trolley can correct an orientation (H3):

| trolley off by | yaw (about vertical) | pitch (about horizontal) | band skew over the 8 mm band |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25° | 1.88 mm | 1.87 mm | 0.026 mm |
| 0.50° | 1.86 | 1.82 | 0.052 |
| **1.00°** | **1.81** | **1.74** | **0.105** |
| 2.00° | 1.71 | 1.58 | 0.209 |
| 3.00° | 1.61 | **1.41** | 0.314 |
| 5.00° | 1.41 | 1.09 | 0.523 |

**Rotation is loose and translation is tight.** About **2°** before the
reserve is touched, nothing contacts out to 5°, and the band skew at 1° is
0.105 mm against the band's own 0.444 mm pitch — irrelevant.

That split is exactly what a coarse-fine stage delivers: the fine screws fix
translation to `REPEAT = 0.05 mm`, and rotation only has to be roughly
right. **The architecture and the tolerance budget agree**, which is the
result that makes this worth doing.

---

### H3. The fine assembly gets a yaw stage: four degrees of freedom out of six

**The trolley's position does not have to be good, and the fine assembly has
to be able to fix its orientation.** The first draft of this part got both
halves wrong — it invented a ±25 mm accuracy requirement for the mecanum
base that is not a requirement, and then proposed a passive straightedge to
hold the base's yaw mechanically. **Both are withdrawn.**

| the trolley is off in | who fixes it | how well |
|---|---|---|
| x, y | the two fine stages | **exactly** — `REPEAT = 0.05 mm` |
| z | the Z lead screw | **exactly** |
| **yaw** (about vertical) | **a fine yaw stage** | **exactly**, to its own resolution |
| **pitch** (nose up/down) | the stages take the **displacement**; the tilt stays | residual **0.17 mm** at 1° |
| **roll** (about the chord) | **nothing needed** | **0.00 mm** to 3° |

**The three rotations are three different cases, and an earlier draft of
this part lumped the last two together as "floor flatness; absorbed".** That
was pessimistic and it hid the two facts that matter. Measured over all 30
joints, against the 1.91 mm clearance a perfect pose gives:

| tilt | yaw | pitch | **roll** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1° | 1.81 mm | 1.74 mm | **1.91 mm** |
| 2° | 1.71 | 1.58 | **1.91** |
| 3° | 1.61 | 1.41 | **1.91** |
| 5° | 1.41 | 1.09 | 1.69 |

**ROLL IS FREE, and it is free for a reason worth knowing.** Roll is a
rotation about the ring's *own* axis, so it does not tilt the ring's plane
at all — it only turns the ring within that plane, which moves where the gap
points. The gap is **60° wide** and `gap_azimuth` parks it at ±10°, so it
carries slack the work never uses, and three degrees of roll disappears into
that slack. **Do not try to compensate it**: re-parking the gap by the roll
angle costs 0.12 mm at 1° and 0.45 mm at 3°, in *either* direction, because
the solver's azimuth is already a local optimum and moving it either way
walks off the peak.

**PITCH'S DOMINANT EFFECT IS A DISPLACEMENT, AND THE STAGES CANCEL IT
EXACTLY.** A trolley pitching about its wheel contact line swings a ring
standing `h` above that line through `h·θ`:

| ring height above the wheels | 0.5° | 1.0° |
|---|---|---|
| 300 mm | 2.62 mm | 5.24 mm |
| **400 mm** | 3.49 mm | **6.98 mm** |
| 500 mm | 4.36 mm | 8.73 mm |

Seven millimetres at one degree — **fifteen times the 0.45 mm translation
budget**, and the largest single error in the whole architecture. It is also
*exactly* correctable, because it is a translation: measure the pitch, and
X, Y and Z null it. What is left after that is the tilt alone, and the tilt
costs **0.17 mm** of the 1.91.

So the rule is: **measure all six, null four, and the two left over cost
0.17 mm and nothing.** A translation cannot change an orientation — that part
stands — but for pitch the orientation was never the expensive half.

**The mecanum's job collapses to one sentence: get the joint inside the
fine stages' reach** — 361 × 361 mm on 500 mm screws (I2), which leaves
±132 mm of park error even when two joints must share one park. Nothing
else about how it arrives matters.

**Where it goes in the stack.** Above X, Y and Z, rotating the head alone,
so that X and Y stay aligned to the truss and their commands need no frame
transform. Put its axis **as near the ring's centre as the packaging
allows**: rotating about a point offset from the ring displaces the ring by
the lever arm, and while that is a known constant the XY stages then cancel,
a short arm is less to cancel and less to calibrate.

**What it has to be.**

| | requirement | why |
|---|---|---|
| range | **±10°** | far more than a park needs; costs nothing |
| resolution | **≤0.05°** | 1° costs 0.10 mm of a 1.91 mm clearance (H2), so this is 20× inside it |
| **backlash** | **low or preloaded** | the one that matters: a closed loop removes *error*, not *hysteresis* |
| holding | self-locking or braked | it must not creep through an 18.5 s wind |

Two forms fit. A **worm and wheel** is compact and self-locking, and the
cell already drives the cage with one — but a worm has backlash unless it is
preloaded. A **lever and lead screw** (a sine stage) has none worth the name
once a spring preloads it, reuses the Tr8 screw family E2 just adopted, and
on a 100 mm arm gives **0.0057° per full step** with ±17.6 mm of travel
covering ±10°. For a ±10° correction axis the lever is the better answer.

**Its duty is per PARK, not per joint.** The trolley does not move between
the joints of one park, so yaw is set once per park — about **15 times per
truss**, against the ring's 30 winds. It is a set-and-hold axis, not a
contouring one, which is why a lever with limited range is enough.

**The actuator count returns to nine, and it is a different nine.** The
dispenser left (PART F) and a yaw stage arrived: X, Y, Z, **yaw**, ring,
cage, gripper stroke, gripper yaw, jaws. What the swap buys is the deletion
of the 1400 mm rail and ball screw, and with it PART B (H1). The head's yaw
and the gripper's yaw are kept separate on purpose — one wants ±10° with
0.05° resolution, the other wants a large range to orient a rod, and making
one actuator do both makes the rod-handling axis the precision element.

**Stop at yaw; do not chase pitch and roll with actuators.** A roll axis
would correct nothing (roll already costs nothing), and a pitch axis would
buy 0.17 mm of clearance at 1° — a residual already inside the reserve —
while the 7 mm that pitch *actually* costs is taken out by stages that have
to be there anyway. If a floor ever did make pitch matter, the answer is
still not an actuator but **three levelling screws on the trolley**, set
once and static.

**What pitch and roll do require is MEASUREMENT.** Not to correct the tilt,
but because pitch's 7 mm displacement cannot be nulled without knowing the
angle — which is the second reason H4 insists the tags are spread rather
than single. A trolley whose pitch is unknown is 7 mm out and does not know
it; a trolley whose pitch is measured is 0.17 mm out and does.

### H4. What the vision has to deliver, and what actually limits it

A 250 mm baseline at a 300 mm working distance, with f ≈ 1100 px and 0.2 px
of disparity noise, gives σ ≈ **0.065 mm in depth and 0.054 mm laterally**
**(estimated from σ_Z = Z²σ_d / fB; not measured on this rig)** — comparable
to the ball screws' own `REPEAT = 0.05 mm` and far inside the 0.45 mm
budget. **The sensor is not the limit**, and `Vision` already recorded what
is:

> *"what limits the cell is the camera-to-ring calibration, which is a
> bracket, and it is drawn ONCE per run as a bias, never averaged away."*

**Three consequences that decide the build:**

1. **The camera mounts on the fine stage, next to the ring — not on the
   world.** Fixed to the world, the camera-to-ring transform depends on the
   fine stage's position, which is the very thing being measured. On the
   stage, the bracket is short, stiff, and the loop closes on a relative
   measurement.
2. **SPREAD THE TAGS. Do not localise off one.** A single planar fiducial
   gives a well-conditioned *position* and an ill-conditioned *orientation*
   — the planar pose ambiguity — which is precisely the half H3 says cannot
   be corrected and therefore has to be measured well. Several widely
   separated tags turn orientation into triangulation between distant
   points: at ~0.05 mm per point and a 300 mm spread, yaw resolves to about
   **0.015°** **(estimated: sigma*sqrt(2)/S)**, and the ambiguity disappears
   because the tags are no longer coplanar-and-small. Printing four tags
   instead of one is free.
3. **The work needs a background.** Pultruded carbon rod is black and
   specular: poor texture for stereo matching. The existing spec sidesteps
   this by fitting **edges** against a background — *"20 pixels across a
   3 mm chord and an edge fit resolves a hundredth of a millimetre"* — so
   give the work a contrasting or backlit backdrop. A sheet of white
   acrylic, and it is free.

---

### H5. Does the trolley stay put while it winds?

The loads are negligible and worth having the numbers for. The ring's peak
reaction is **0.049 N·m** (`check_drive`), which across a 300 mm-wide trolley
is **0.33 N** at the wheels; the thread pulls **2 N** at r 11 mm. Static
friction on four mecanum wheels holds that without thinking about it, so
**no clamp or jack is needed for load.**

What does need attention is **drift**: unpowered drive motors back-drive,
and a wheel that creeps takes the ring's *orientation* with it, which H3
says nothing on the trolley can correct. Holding current on the four wheel
motors, or a detent — the same problem E2 just deleted on Z, reappearing on
the base, and here it costs orientation rather than position.

---

### H6. What it buys, what it costs, and what it reverses

**Buys:** no 1400 mm rail or ball screw — the most expensive and least
procurable item in the BOM; PART B becomes moot (H1); two **500 mm** stages
instead of a 1400 and a 300; the mecanum base and the stereo rig are already
owned. **A 500 mm screw leaves 361 mm of stroke** once its ends, supports
and nut are subtracted, which covers **four joint pitches** (97.7 mm each)
plus the park error — about **3 coarse parks per chord, 9 per truss**, not
30. (An earlier draft said 250 mm and 15 parks. It used the nominal length
as the stroke: 250 mm nominal is 111 mm of travel and covers **one** joint,
which is a park per joint and throws the architecture away. See I2.)

**Costs:**

- **A closed loop where there is none.** The HAL is open-loop step counting
  today, and `check_hal` asserts exactly that: an axis *believes the
  quantised setpoint*. Closed-loop fine axes plus a coarse pose is a real
  change to `cell.py`, not a parameter.
- **Hold current on the four wheels** (H5), because a creeping wheel
  costs orientation, not just position.
- **A fourth fine axis: yaw** (H3). This is the swap that makes the
  architecture work, and it is what the 1400 mm rail's deletion is traded
  for.
- **Vision stops being optional.** In the gantry architecture the fixture's
  ±1 mm was survivable; here vision is the only thing that closes the loop.

**So H reverses E3.** E3 deferred the camera because the fixture alone
survived at 0.95 mm of clearance. Under H, deferring it is not available —
the coarse base's ±25 mm has to be measured away. E3's mitigation (cut the
rods on a length stop) is still worth doing, but it no longer substitutes
for the camera.

---

### H7. What has to change in the simulation

1. `Gantry.X_TRAVEL` and `Y_TRAVEL` become **fine-stage** travels (250 each),
   and the plan gains a **coarse pose per park** — about 15 of them.
2. A **coarse-park op** with its settling time, followed by a **register**
   step: measure the trolley's pose from the tags, then null x, y, z on the
   linear stages and yaw on the rotary one. No retry and no convergence
   criterion — with a yaw stage the correction is commanded once, not
   iterated, which is the second thing the stage buys.
3. The HAL grows a **`measure()`** returning the camera's estimate, and the
   fine axes' `goto` becomes closed-loop. `check_hal`'s "no ground-truth
   reads in mission code" ratchet already admits a camera as a *sensor*, so
   the contract survives — but it has to be written.
4. **PART B's C1–C10 can be dropped** if H1 holds.
5. `check_approach` gains the two tables above as assertions rather than
   one-off measurements: the margin must survive ±0.45 mm and ±2°.

Nothing here is done. Items 1–3 are the real work and they are larger than
the removals in G3, because they *add* a feedback path rather than deleting
an actuator.

## PART I — THE PARTS LIST, REVISED

**This supersedes section 1 (Linear Motion) and parts of sections 2 and 3 at
the top of this document.** The original list is left in place because PART A
reviews it and the history is worth keeping; order from here.

---

### I1. Do you need two rails for X and one for Y? No — one rail and two blocks, on all three

**The rail count is set by an angle, not by a weight**, and the angle is
generous. The translation budget at the ring is **0.45 mm** (H2), and the
ring stands roughly 400 mm above the X carriage, so any angular give in the
stack is amplified by that lever:

| ring above the carriage | angular give allowed |
|---|---|
| 300 mm | 1.500 mrad (0.086°) |
| **400 mm** | **1.125 mrad (0.064°)** |
| 500 mm | 0.900 mrad (0.052°) |

And the moment that has to be resisted is small. Three kilos above the X
carriage **(estimated)** at `A_MAX["x"] = 400 mm/s²` is 1.2 N, which at
400 mm is 0.480 N·m; the ring's own peak reaction adds 0.049. Worst case
**0.53 N·m**.

> **The stack needs better than 470 N·m/rad.** An MGN12H block carries a
> static moment rating of order 20 N·m at a deflection of tens of microns,
> which puts its stiffness four decades above that **(estimated — not from a
> datasheet)**. One rail with two spaced blocks clears the bar by roughly
> 40×.

**So: one MGN12 rail with two blocks on each of X, Y and Z.** Two parallel
rails would be stiffer, but they also have to be made parallel *and*
coplanar, and on a precision stage that alignment is the hard part of the
build. A single rail has no alignment to get wrong. If a first article turns
out floppy, drill the plate for the second rail then — the stiffness can be
added later, the wasted alignment effort cannot be taken back.

This is the opposite of the original list, and correctly so: **that list was
for a 1.4 m gantry bridge**, where two rails carry a spanning beam and stop
it racking. A 250 mm cross-slide on a trolley plate is not that machine.

---

### I2. 500 mm, not 250 — and the nominal length is not the travel

**A nominal screw length is not a stroke.** The machined ends, the two end
supports and the nut's own body all come off it before anything moves.
Taking a 500 mm SFU1204 with BK10/BF10 and a flange nut **(dimensions
estimated from catalogue geometry, not measured)**: 45 mm of machined ends,
34 for the BK10, 20 for the BF10, 40 for the nut — **125 mm gone.**

Against a joint pitch of **97.7 mm** and ten joints per chord:

| nominal | usable travel | joints per park | **parks per truss** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 250 mm | 111 mm | **1** | **30** |
| 300 mm | 161 mm | 2 | 15 |
| 400 mm | 261 mm | 3 | 12 |
| **500 mm** | **361 mm** | **4** | **9** |

**So 500 mm is not a procurement convenience, it is the right length, and
the 250 mm in the first draft of this part was wrong.** At 250 mm nominal
the stage has 111 mm of stroke, which after any park error covers **one
joint** — a park per joint, which throws away most of what the coarse-fine
split was for. PART H's claim that "a 250 mm fine X covers two joint
pitches" used the nominal length as the stroke and is corrected below.

A 500 mm screw also makes the mecanum's job trivial: if two joints per park
is enough, the park may be **±132 mm** out.

**Z stays short.** `Z_TRAVEL` is 150 mm, Z is a Tr8×2 lead screw rather than
an SFU (E2), and lead screws are sold in any length — so Z needs no 500 mm
part and should not have one. A 500 mm Z column would raise the whole
machine and lengthen the very lever that amplifies every angular error (I1).

| Qty | Item | Spec | For |

| **2** | MGN12 linear rail, **500 mm** | with **2 × MGN12H blocks each** | X, Y |
| **1** | MGN12 linear rail, **300–400 mm** | with 2 blocks | Z |

### I2a. What the screw combo does NOT include, and this was the right question

The `RKI-1736` listing is **the screw rod and the flange nut. That is all.**
Nothing in it touches the plate. What has to go with it:

| | needed? | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| **BK10** (fixed end) | **yes** | holds an angular-contact bearing pair, takes the axial thrust, and **its base is what bolts the screw to the plate**. The machined ends exist for this |
| **BF10** (floated end) | **yes** | a plain bearing that lets the screw grow with temperature. Bolt both ends rigidly and a warm screw buckles or preloads itself |
| **motor mount** | **yes** | holds the NEMA 17 in line, usually bolting to the BK10's face |
| **coupler, 5 → 8 mm** | **yes** | the SFU1204's machined end is 8 mm; the motor's shaft is 5 |
| **DSG12H nut housing** | **no — optional** | **a *flange* nut already has a bolted flange, so it mounts straight to the carriage plate.** The housing is for when you want the nut at a different height or orientation. An earlier draft of this part listed it as required; it is not |
| **a drilled and tapped plate** | **yes** | the rail's counterbores, the BK/BF bases and the nut's flange all land on it. This is the "Carriage Adaptor Plate" in section 5 of the original list, and it is the part you have to have made |

**And the assembly order matters more than any of the parts**, because
getting it wrong quietly ruins a ball nut:

> Mount the **rail** first. Run the **carriage** onto it. Bolt the **nut's
> flange** to the carriage. Only then bolt **BK10 and BF10** down, with the
> carriage run to each end in turn, so the screw finds its own line parallel
> to the rail. A screw bolted down first and a nut then forced onto it binds
> for the life of the machine.

### I2b. Check the trolley deck before ordering

A 500 mm X stage carrying a 500 mm Y stage occupies a footprint of roughly
**560 × 560 mm** once the end supports are counted, and the moving Y plate
sweeps a good part of that **(estimated from the part lengths, not laid
out)**. That has to sit on the mecanum robot's deck with the truss's fixture
somewhere it can reach. **Measure the robot's deck against 600 × 600 mm
before the parcel arrives** — this is the one dimension in PART H that has
never been drawn, and it is cheaper to find now than after the screws are
cut.

### I6. What this is worth checking before the parcel arrives

One number in I1 is an estimate and it is the one the whole rail decision
rests on: **an MGN12H block's moment stiffness**. The requirement is
470 N·m/rad and the claim is that a block beats it by ~40×, from a rating
rather than a stiffness figure. If a supplier's datasheet gives a real
deflection-under-moment curve, read it — and if it does not, the first
article settles it in a minute: mount the stack, hang a known weight on the
head at a known lever, and measure the tilt. 0.064° is 0.28 mm at a 250 mm
dial gauge, which is easy to see.
